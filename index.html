How the model works

The storage can be pictured as a tank with four compartments. What comes in is produced by the beamlines; what goes out depends on the usage rules. The diagram shows what the simulation does on average, and each quantity is a piece you can change with the controls.

scroll sideways to see the whole diagram
1

Input

Every week the beamlines send proposals. The volume grows, doubling every Tf (“future growth”). The processed data are a fraction of the raw data, capped by the quota.

JD(t) = JD(0) · 2t/Tf
JP(t) ≈ r̄ JD(t),   proc/ per proposal ≤ Q
in the widget: the drops; the blue “input” curve in the flow chart
2

Retention is a delay

What expires today is what came in R weeks ago. The amount on disk does not matter: the outflow only looks at age.

dSdt = JD(t) − JD(t − R)
in the widget: the dashed “expiring” curve
3

Queue and tape

Expired raw data join the queue, and the drives write at most W per week. If more expires than the tape can write, the queue grows.

dFdt = JD(t − R) − φw
φw = min{ W − φb, F }
W = ndrives · η · 242 TB/week
in the widget: the wide pipe, the hatched band (“by stock”) and the “Tape queue” card
4

Recalls from tape

Recall requests go through a narrow pipe, arrive after a delay L and use the same drives. The data stay on disk for 8 weeks and then leave; the copy remains on tape.

dBdt = φb(t) − φb(t − 8)
φb = min{ Wb, requests(t − L) }
in the widget: the narrow pipe, the small cart and the “Recall requests” card
5

proc/ never goes to tape

Processed data can be recomputed: at the end of the retention period they are deleted (after a notice). Without a usage rule, they only leave in an emergency cleanup.

dPdt = JP(t) − JP(t − R) − c(t)
(c = emergency cleanup, card 6) · in the widget: the light colour in the beamline rectangles
6

Occupation and limit

All beamlines share the same synchrotron capacity V. Above the limit α (default 90%, editable), the largest proc/ directories are deleted first until the occupation is 3 points below it. The purchase alert fires 1 year before α is crossed.

U = S + F + B + P ≤ V
U > αV ⇒ emergency cleanup
in the widget: the red line and the “Emergency cleanups” card

The complete system

The equations of the cards hold for each beamline ℓ = 1, …, 22, each with its own input. The beamlines only meet in the resources they share: the tape and the disk. This is where a heavy beamline can squeeze the others.

for each beamline ℓ
dSℓdt = JD,ℓ(t) − JD,ℓ(t − R)
dFℓdt = JD,ℓ(t − R) − φw,ℓ(t)
dBℓdt = φb,ℓ(t) − φb,ℓ(t − 8)
dPℓdt = JP,ℓ(t) − JP,ℓ(t − R) − cℓ(t)
what is shared (couples the beamlines)
Σℓ [ φw,ℓ + φb,ℓ ] ≤ W,   Σℓ φb,ℓ ≤ Wb
U = Σℓ ( Sℓ + Fℓ + Bℓ + Pℓ ) ≤ V
cℓ(t) > 0 only if U > αV

The tape serves the queue of all beamlines together, oldest data first. The emergency cleanup deletes the largest proc/ directories first, whatever their beamline.

inputs and special cases
JD,ℓ(t) = JD,ℓ(0) · 2t/Tf
JP,ℓ: proc/ of each proposal ≤ Qk(ℓ)

Without a usage rule: R = ∞, Q = ∞, nothing goes to tape. What remains is dUdt = Σℓ Jℓ − Σℓ cℓ: only the emergency cleanup removes anything.

Summing over beamlines gives back the equations of the cards, with J = Σℓ Jℓ.

Why a delay equation and not a simple ODE

The classical way to model a tank with inflow and outflow is the chemostat: a bioreactor that receives fresh medium and loses, at every instant, a fraction of its content (the same logic as SIR epidemic models). This was the starting point of this model. Its outflow is proportional to the stock, a first-order delay: every byte has the same chance of leaving at any moment, whatever its age, and the fraction that remains decays exponentially (red), with mean residence time R:

dNdt = J(t) − NR

A retention rule does not work like that. It is a step, a pipeline delay (green): nothing leaves before R, everything leaves at R. With the same mean R, a first-order delay would remove recent data too early and keep old data forever. Hence the outflow today is the inflow R weeks ago: a delay equation, not an ODE. The tape bottleneck (min{W, F}) and the 90% trigger have no counterpart in the chemostat either.

How the simulation generates the data

  • Proposal by proposal, week by week. The equations above are the average of this.
  • Each beamline receives a random number of proposals per week. The mean matches, in TB/week, the measured rate of the beamline; the profile sets the typical proposal size. The mean grows over time.
  • Each proposal has a random raw size and a proc/ that grows over ~6 weeks; sizes and proc/÷raw ratios per profile come from the real proposals (commissioning proposals are larger, with inflated proc/).
  • The past is generated so that the history reproduces today's usage of the synchrotron share (3.24 PB by default: beamlines + general, split among the beamlines).
  • Recall requests are drawn per proposal on tape, with the weekly chance set in the assumptions.
  • Week 0 is today: both tanks start from the same state, and new proposals and the usage rules act from week 1 on.
  • 20 futures are simulated; the band in the occupation chart covers 80% of them.
